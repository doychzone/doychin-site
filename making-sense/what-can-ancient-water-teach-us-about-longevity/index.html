What Can Ancient Water Teach Us About Longevity?

From the Roman Thermae in Varna to Rome, and one opportunity for thermal destinations.

Wednesday, September 16, 2026About a 4 minute 30 second read

The Roman Thermae of ancient Odessos in Varna, my hometown. Nearly 1,800 years of history, with the modern city continuing behind them.

Good morning, Sensemakers!

I took the photo above in Varna, my hometown.

On the left are the remains of the Roman Thermae of ancient Odessos. On the right are the buildings of the modern city.

Nearly 1,800 years in one frame.

The Roman Thermae were built at the end of the second century AD. They covered more than 7,000 square metres, making them the largest Roman baths discovered in the Balkans and the fourth largest in Europe.

Some of the preserved walls still rise more than 20 metres above the ground.

This was not simply a place where people washed.

The complex included hot, warm and cold rooms, spaces for exercise, rest and conversation. Marble, coloured mosaics and carefully designed heating and water systems turned bathing into a complete experience.

Today’s issue is about one idea:

We can use modern science and technology to make an ancient practice more personal, useful and effective.

1. What did the Romans understand?

Water was part of Roman public life.

They invested enormous resources in aqueducts, baths, fountains and systems for moving, heating and managing water.

An inscription discovered in Varna tells us that during the reign of Emperor Antoninus Pius, the city and the Roman governor brought water to Odessos through an aqueduct.

A few days ago, during my trip to Rome, I kept seeing evidence of the same respect.

At the Baths of Caracalla, water arrived through a dedicated branch of one of Rome’s great aqueducts.

Visitors moved through rooms with different temperatures. They exercised, received body care, walked, met other people and spent time reading or studying.

The Romans did not have wearable technology, modern laboratories or our current understanding of human biology.

But they understood that water, temperature, movement, recovery and social connection belonged together.

We should also be precise.

Roman baths are not scientific proof that thermal or mineral water extends life. Many of them were supplied by aqueducts and the water was heated.

Their real lesson is different. They developed systems around water because they recognised its importance for health, restoration and everyday life.

2. What does the evidence tell us today?

Modern science gives us reasons to take thermal mineral water seriously, but not to turn it into another exaggerated longevity promise.

Systematic reviews of clinical trials report that thermal mineral water treatments can reduce pain and improve physical function and quality of life in people with osteoarthritis and other musculoskeletal conditions.

A large observational study following more than 30,000 adults in Japan found that frequent hot bathing was associated with a lower risk of cardiovascular disease.

Associated does not mean caused.

The study was about hot bathing, not specifically mineral water. It does not prove that taking more baths will make us live longer.

Research also suggests that warm water bathing may support sleep and relaxation.

At the same time, not every study finds improvements in every health marker. Some studies are small. The quality of the evidence varies.

So the honest conclusion is not: Thermal water guarantees longevity.

There is a meaningful signal, and we now have better ways to understand it.

What kind of water was used? At what temperature? For how long? How did the person respond? What changed afterwards?

Mineral water is not magic. Heat is not harmless. Longer is not always better.

The details matter.

3. Where do we go from here?

Many hotels, SPAs and thermal destinations already have something valuable: mineral water, thermal pools, established procedures, experienced therapists and a strong connection to their location and history.

What is often missing is a clear way to understand how the individual person responds.

Technology can help.

We can begin with the person’s condition, needs and resources. We can record the water composition, temperature, duration and frequency of the procedure.

We can observe relevant changes in heart rate, sleep, recovery, pain, movement and perceived wellbeing. Then we can use the response to adjust the next experience.

Not to replace the tradition. To understand it better.

Technology should not turn a thermal experience into a laboratory. It should quietly help the experience become safer, more personal and more useful.

Not ancient or modern.

The best of both, used with common sense.

One useful step this week
Before your next thermal or mineral water experience, ask one question:

How will I know whether this was useful for me?

That question changes the experience.

It moves us from assumption to attention. From a procedure offered to everyone to an experience that can begin to learn from the individual.

Was this useful?

Making Sense of Community

Your people are part of your longevity plan. So is showing up.

Wednesday, November 18, 2026About a 7 minute read

Good morning, Sensemakers!

For most of this year, most of the people I learn from and talk with the most have lived inside my phone. Messages, voice notes, threads, a comment here and there.

Last night, that changed. I sat in the same room with people, most of whom I had never met in person, and our only connection was a small community we belong to. We went around the table telling our stories, getting to know each other and understanding a little more deeply what moves the person next to us. It was a quiet and often a little weird gathering for a recovering introvert like me, but looking at the photos now, I think we had fun and left with a few new connections that make sense.

This made me think about something we usually leave out of our longevity plans. Not just the few people who will pick up when we call (Who Will Pick Up When You Call?), but the wider circle around us. The groups we consciously choose to belong to, and whether we actually show up.

Today, we will make sense of:
  • Why belonging and showing up for a community matters for a long and healthy life
  • How to take an honest look at the circles you are part of
  • Why showing up may be the minimum of community, the same way a vote is the minimum of democracy, or a morning squat the minimum of movement and exercise

But first…

On Tuesday evening, I went to the Stanford Alumni Club Bulgaria. We met at a newly opened restaurant that belongs to a client and friend. The occasion was the club's third anniversary.

I knew many of the people there. Some of them helped me with my TED talk, others came to my book signings or other events. For most of the year, we had talked mostly through online messages. And then there they were. Real voices. Real handshakes.

What surprised me was that this setting allowed each of us to tell a story a little different from our CVs. A former government official shared her passion for dancing. A man I knew from before surprised me with his pursuit of legacy, a project about regenerating soils and encouraging sustainable farming.

I have nothing against messages. They kept us connected all year, across time zones and busy weeks. But something happens in a room that does not happen in a thread. I felt connected, and not in some cheesy way. It reminded me of how I feel watching Jimmy Kimmel: that warm reassurance that the world is still great and full of amazing, funny and well meaning people. For years now, in the darkest moments, when the world seems to go down with another ridiculous piece of news, I have had this routine of watching Jimmy Kimmel's monologue with my brunch. I felt the same hope and connectedness last night.

On the way home, I asked myself a simple question:

So what if I am connected to many people online, if I rarely show up anywhere in person, or if I fail to show up and contribute to the community?

Then I thought this might be a good exercise to run through the Making Sense three questions. It looks like a pretty light topic, after we have covered aliens (Where Is Everybody?), mass tragedies (Can We Make Sense of a Tragedy?) and more in previous issues. So here it goes.

1. Why does this matter?

Why does belonging to a community matter (to me)?

In Who Will Pick Up When You Call? we looked at close relationships. The few people you can call when life gets hard. This issue is about the wider circle around us. A club, a team, a congregation, a neighborhood, a group chat that sometimes becomes a dinner table.

The science here is surprisingly consistent.

A large meta analysis in PLOS Medicine pooled 148 studies with 308,849 participants. People with stronger social relationships had a 50% greater likelihood of survival over the follow up periods. And here is the part I find most interesting for this issue: the link was strongest when researchers measured social integration in a fuller way, how woven in a person is across several relationships and roles, not just whether they live alone. Belonging seems to count, not only closeness.

The US Surgeon General's 2023 advisory, "Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation," puts it plainly. Loneliness and social isolation increase the risk of premature death by 26% and 29%. More broadly, it says, lacking social connection can increase that risk as much as smoking up to 15 cigarettes a day.

The Harvard Study of Adult Development, one of the longest studies of adult life, began in 1938 and followed its participants for decades. Its director, Robert Waldinger, summarized one of its findings like this: "The people who were the most satisfied in their relationships at age 50 were the healthiest at age 80."

And then there are the Blue Zones. One of their "Power 9" habits is called the right tribe. In Okinawa, people formed moais, small groups of friends who committed to each other for life. It is a beautiful idea, and I like it.

I also want to be honest with you. The Blue Zones age data has serious critics. Demographer Saul Justin Newman argues that many extreme age records reflect clerical errors and even pension fraud, and his work won an Ig Nobel Prize in 2024. So I would not lean on Blue Zones as proof. I would treat the moai as an inspiring example, not as evidence.

So what? Most of this research is observational. It shows strong links, not a guarantee. One club membership does not add years to your life.

But the signal keeps showing up, across many studies and many countries. Maybe community belongs in the longevity plan, next to movement, sleep and food.

2. Where am I?

Where am I in all this?

Here is a number that stopped me. The Surgeon General's advisory reports that the time Americans spent with friends in person fell from about 30 hours a month in 2003 to about 10 hours a month in 2020.

So what? We may have more ways to reach each other than ever, and still spend less time in the same room.

I recognize myself in that. A lot of my community lives online. It is real, it is valuable, and it is also easy to mistake for the whole thing.

So where are you? Try these questions, quickly and honestly:

Belong. Which groups am I part of right now? Not the ones I joined once. The ones I would actually miss.

Show up. In which of them have I been present, in person or live, in the last month?

Contribute. Where am I only a member, and where do I give something? Time, help, a question, a welcome to someone new.

Online and offline. Which of my online connections have I never met, and would like to?

There is no right score here. Some seasons of life are quieter. But a clear picture is better than a vague feeling.

3. What can I do?

How can I move, even a little, from being connected to actually belonging?

This is where "So what?" turns into "Now what?"

In The Secret Power of Your… Minimum I wrote about two prescriptions. The optimum is what I do when life allows. The minimum is what I refuse to lose when life doesn't.

In Making Sense of Democracy, I called a vote the minimum of democratic participation. I wrote about helping my friends close the Bulgarian polling station in Cincinnati, and that this is how a community works too.

I think community has a minimum as well. It is showing up.

Not organizing. Not leading. Not being the most active member. Just being there, regularly enough that people notice when you are missing. The optimum is more: helping, hosting, bringing someone new.

A few small ways to start:

  • Go to one gathering you would normally skip.
  • Turn one online conversation into a coffee, a walk or a live call.
  • Arrive early or stay late once, and help set up or clean up.
  • Say yes to one small role, even a tiny one.

One useful step this week

Pick one group you already belong to, online or offline. Answer the three questions, one sentence each:

  1. Why does it matter to me? What would I miss if it disappeared?
  2. Where am I? When did I last show up there, really?
  3. What can I do? What is my minimum, one appearance this week, even in a busy week? Put it in your calendar now.

Then go. Missed it? Go the next time.

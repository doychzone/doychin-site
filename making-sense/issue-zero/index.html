What Kind of 80-Year-Old Are You Preparing to Become?

Clarity in a noisy world.

Wednesday, August 12, 2026About a 4 minute read

Good morning, Sensemakers!

Welcome to Issue Zero of Making Sense.

For almost three decades, I have watched people begin exercising because they want to change how they look.

Eventually, a more important question appears:

What do I want my body to remain capable of doing?

Climbing stairs. Carrying luggage. Getting up from the floor. Traveling, working, and playing without constantly negotiating with the body.

That is when exercise stops being a cosmetic project and becomes preparation for freedom.

But first…

What kind of 80-year-old are you preparing to become?

Not someday. Now.

The answer is not hidden in a single blood test, workout, device, or expert opinion. It is being shaped quietly, in whether you can get up from the floor, carry a bag, sleep without fear of your sleep score, and keep doing the things that make life feel like your own.

This is the point of Making Sense. No noise. No empty claims. Just better questions, clearer evidence, and practical ways to protect the life inside your years.

Three things worth understanding

1. Muscle is your independence fund

Most people think of strength training as something for appearance, athletic performance, or youth. I see it differently.

Muscle is useful tissue. It helps you stand up, climb stairs, catch yourself when you lose balance, lift a suitcase, recover after illness, and remain capable in your own home.

That is why I think of muscle as an independence fund.

Every sensible training session is a small deposit. You may not need it today. But one day, you will be grateful that you made it.

Research consistently finds that muscular strength and resistance training are associated with lower risk of early death. That does not mean one gym session buys you extra years. It means strength is not cosmetic. It is one of the clearest signals of physical reserve.

The practical point
You do not need an extreme programme. Start by training the major muscle groups at least twice a week, at a level appropriate for you.

2. Your sleep tracker is a clue, not a verdict

A bad sleep score can ruin a morning before the day has even begun. That is too much power to give a wristwatch.

Consumer trackers are useful. They can help us notice patterns in bedtime, wake time, movement, and sometimes recovery trends. But they do not directly measure the sleeping brain.

Clinical sleep studies use brain activity, eye movements, muscle activity, breathing, and more. Your tracker estimates sleep from signals such as movement and heart rate.

A recent meta-analysis found meaningful differences between consumer wrist-worn devices and clinical sleep measurement for several key sleep parameters. Use the data as a prompt for curiosity, not as a diagnosis or a daily judgement of your wellbeing.

The practical point
Look for patterns across weeks. If you feel rested, function well, and your habits support sleep, do not let one disappointing score tell you otherwise.

3. Creatine is a supporting actor, not the hero

Creatine has spent too long trapped in the bodybuilder aisle.

It is one of the most studied sports supplements, and research suggests it may support strength and lean tissue, particularly alongside resistance training. This makes it interesting well beyond sport.

But this is where the wellness world often loses the plot.

A supplement cannot create the benefit of movement. It cannot replace protein, sleep, recovery, or a programme you can actually sustain.

Creatine may support the work. It is not the work.

The practical point
If you are considering creatine, begin with the foundation first. Regular strength training, enough food, enough protein, and recovery. If you have kidney disease or another condition requiring medical monitoring, discuss supplements with your clinician first.

Making sense of it

These three stories are really one story.

Do not outsource your future to a number, a device, or a powder. Build capacity.

Train because you want to stay free. Use technology because it helps you notice, not because it tells you who you are. Use supplements only when they support a life that is already moving in the right direction.

This week’s experiment

Choose one action that your future self would thank you for.

Two strength sessions. A regular bedtime. A walk after dinner. A little more protein at breakfast.

Make one deposit into your independence fund.

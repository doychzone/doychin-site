Making Sense of Gratitude

The subtle power of sealing a moment and savouring it for years to come.

Wednesday, November 25, 2026About a 6 minute read

Good morning, Sensemakers!

Tomorrow is Thanksgiving here in America. It's a natural day to talk about gratitude. But gratitude isn't a holiday habit. It's one of the simplest ways we notice who carried us, and keep a good moment close enough to reopen later.

Last week, in Making Sense of Community, I wrote about sitting in the same room with people instead of living inside my phone. This issue is quieter. It's about giving thanks, receiving it, and sealing a moment so you can feel it again years later.

Today, we will make sense of:
  • Why both giving and receiving gratitude show up in the research on health and longevity
  • Why we hold back from saying a real thank you, even though it lands better than we think
  • How to seal a meaningful moment so you can reopen it years later

But first…

I've often written that Thanksgiving is my favorite American holiday. I still remember our first one here, in 2003: friends, a turkey next to Bulgarian dishes, and help with a strange idiom I had no way of knowing (what the heck is a rain check?).

Two years ago on Thanksgiving I was in Bulgaria. I had arrived urgently. My mother was bedridden.

That first night I made an improvised bed on a sofa by a window, the first bed I made so I could be next to her at night.

She passed away in my arms a few days later.

Today, along with the sealed grief of those days, I remember with gratitude the stolen little words, the touches, and the chance to be with her in her last days. On that Thanksgiving I was completely alone, but I felt the warmth, love and support of everyone around me.

A word. A touch. These are the invisible threads that bring a moment back. That is what I mean by sealing it.

1. Why does this matter?

Why does giving and receiving gratitude matter for health and longevity?

I get skeptical when people call gratitude a "biohack." That word usually means someone is about to sell you a gadget, or promise you the world. Gratitude is free. It's slow. And it's mostly unglamorous. But when researchers actually measure it, something real shows up.

In 2024, a study in JAMA Psychiatry followed nearly 50,000 older US female nurses from the Nurses' Health Study. The women in the highest third of gratitude scores had a lower risk of death, including from heart disease, than those in the lowest third, even after accounting for health, lifestyle and social life. This was an observational study, so it can't prove cause and effect. Still, the signal is hard to ignore.

What I find even more interesting is what happens when gratitude moves between two people.

In a two week diary study of 67 cohabiting couples, Sara Algoe and her colleagues found something simple. When one partner did something thoughtful and the other felt genuine gratitude, not just a sense of owing a favor, both of them felt closer and more satisfied the next day: the one who received the kindness, and the one who gave it.

Receiving thanks changes the giver too. Adam Grant and Francesca Gino tested this in several experiments, including a field study with university fundraisers. A simple thank you made people feel more socially valued and more willing to help again. Being thanked tells us something basic: what I do matters to someone else.

And yet we hold back. Amit Kumar and Nicholas Epley asked people to write gratitude letters and guess how the other person would feel. The writers almost always got it wrong. They thought the other person would feel less glad, and more awkward, than they actually did. In our heads, saying thank you feels a little embarrassing. On the receiving end, it lands like warm sunlight.

As I wrote in Who Will Pick Up When You Call?, social connection belongs in a longevity plan next to sleep, movement and food. Gratitude is one of the simplest ways we strengthen those ties, from both sides of the table.

2. Where am I?

How does gratitude live in my days right now, and how do I keep a good moment from slipping away?

In Why Just Knowing Is Not Enough?, we looked at the gap between knowing what helps and actually doing it. Almost nobody argues against gratitude. And still, plenty of us rush right past the moments worth keeping.

When I reopen those days now, what stays with me is not a summary. It is a few stolen words, a touch, the chance and the honor to be there. Those small details are the invisible threads that bring a moment back years later.

Researchers call this savoring: holding a good experience, and being able to revisit it later. There's a practical reason to try it. When people recall a vivid positive memory after a stressful event, their stress response is quieter and their mood steadies more quickly than when they recall something neutral. A well kept memory isn't just nostalgia. You can reopen it when you need it.

A few honest questions, on a holiday or on an ordinary day:

Notice. Who helped carry me, in a big way or a small one, even something as ordinary as a word, a touch, or a door held open?

Express. Have I actually told them, or did I assume they already know?

Receive. When someone thanks me, do I wave it off ("it was nothing"), or pause for two seconds and let it land?

Seal. When a good moment happens, do I rush to the next thing and my phone, or do I pause long enough to notice the warmth of a hand, a few quiet words, the aroma in the room, the light, the sound of a particular laugh?

3. What can I do?

How do I turn gratitude into something I can feel again later?

You don't need an elaborate ritual. You just need a way to catch one real moment and keep it. (In The Secret Power of Your… Minimum, that's the idea: a small floor you can actually keep.)

To seal a moment so you can reopen it later, don't try to summarize the whole day. Catch one or two details you can pull on years from now:

  • The warmth of the hand you are holding.
  • A few words you would not want to lose.
  • The aroma in the room, the spice, the bread, the coffee.
  • The particular quality of the light, late afternoon on the window or the glow over the table.
  • The sound of one specific laugh, the one you would recognize with your eyes closed.

Write those details down before the day blurs, along with who was there. "Thankful for family" fades fast. "The warmth of a hand, a few quiet words, the sound of that laugh" can bring the whole room back.

When someone thanks you, don't swat it away. Say "you are welcome" and let yourself feel that what you did mattered.

One useful step this week

If this week brings you to a Thanksgiving table, or if it's simply an ordinary day, try two small things:

  1. Say one specific thank you. Pick one person who gave you help, advice, encouragement or a warm welcome. Send three sentences, call them, or tell them in person what they did and why it stayed with you. Ignore the voice saying it will feel awkward. The research suggests they'll be far gladder to hear it than you expect.
  2. Seal one moment to reopen later. Before the day ends, write down the details of one good moment. Not a summary. The warmth of a hand, a few words, the aroma in the room, the light, the sound of a particular laugh, and who was there. Set a reminder for a quieter week later on, open the note, and let those details bring the moment back.
